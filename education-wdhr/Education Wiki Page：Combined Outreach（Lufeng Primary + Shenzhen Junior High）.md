# Education: Friskoli in the Classroom — Two Outreach Programmes

Our SZU-SIAT China iGEM team delivered the same project, **Friskoli** (BioMicroStir), to two very different audiences: elementary school students in rural Lufeng, and junior high students in Shenzhen. The two programmes were deliberately tiered — the primary-school session teaches the *phenomenon* through story and craft, while the junior-high session teaches the *mechanism* through reasoning, a hands-on experiment and a paired survey.

| | Programme 1 | Programme 2 |
|---|---|---|
| School | Lufeng Neihu Town Central Primary School | Shenzhen Experimental School (Junior High Section) |
| Audience | Elementary students | 42 students, Grades 7–8 |
| Depth | Phenomenon: bacteria as story protagonists | Mechanism: chemotaxis, gradients and experimental design |

## Programme 1: Outreach at Lufeng Neihu Town Central Primary School

### Project Overview

Our SZU-SIAT China iGEM team carried out a science popularization and educational outreach activity at Lufeng Neihu Town Central Primary School, targeting elementary school students with a kid-friendly synthetic biology story *Space Garbage Cleaner E. coli*. This lecture combined space science, microgravity knowledge, microbial genetics and hands-on crafts, aiming to spark children's interest in synthetic biology, aerospace engineering and environmental recycling.

### Teaching Narrative & Core Curriculum Design

We built a vivid futuristic story with two cartoon protagonists: astronaut Lili and engineered cleaning E. coli. Set in the year 2049, Lili undertakes a 30-day independent survival mission in a lunar capsule, only carrying food supplies lasting merely 7 days. Genetically modified E. coli is assigned as her exclusive assistant to solve the critical crises emerging during the mission.

#### Lesson 1: Space Waste Crisis & Recycling Demand

We first introduced the real waste situation of the International Space Station. Solid waste including discarded clothes, food residues, tissue and packaging materials is conventionally packed and ejected into orbit, eventually burning up in the atmosphere. This disposal method wastes valuable organic resources, which is a severe problem for long-term lunar survival with limited payload capacity. Lili realizes space organic waste can be recycled as feedstock instead of being discarded.

#### Lesson 2: Microgravity Barrier for Microbe Nutrition Intake

The engineered E. coli fails to access nutrients normally in space and becomes weak and emaciated. We guided students to compare gravity conditions on Earth and in microgravity space:

1. On Earth: natural convection driven by gravity transports nutrient molecules to E. coli continuously.
2. In microgravity: no natural convection occurs; nutrients only spread via slow diffusion, forming a nutrient-depleted zone around bacteria, making it hard for microbes to obtain enough food.

We proposed a preliminary solution — mechanical stirring — yet analyzed its fatal drawbacks for tiny E. coli:

1. High shear force generated by rotating stir blades damages microbial cells;
2. Trapped bubbles block nutrient contact and destabilize reactor pressure without buoyancy;
3. Heat accumulates locally, raising temperature and deactivating bacteria.

#### Lesson 3: Synthetic Biology Solution — Activate the asc Operon

We unveiled our core synthetic biology design: the native *asc* operon inside E. coli, whose expression is normally repressed. By activating this gene circuit, the engineered E. coli gains autonomous chemotaxis to actively seek organic waste nutrients. A positive feedback loop forms: nutrient-degrading bacteria attract more E. coli to aggregate and decompose waste rapidly.

We named our biorecycling system **Friskoli** (blending *Frisk* meaning swimming motility and *coli* for Escherichia coli). Within 7 days, the engineered bacteria fully break down all space organic waste into reusable raw materials like cellobiose, producing fresh nutrients to sustain astronaut Lili's 30-day lunar survival mission.

### Educational Goals & Impact

1. **Popularize interdisciplinary knowledge**: integrate aerospace microgravity, environmental waste recycling and synthetic microbial genetics, breaking the barrier between biology and space engineering for young children.
2. **Demystify E. coli**: eliminate children's fear of E. coli by demonstrating how genetic modification transforms ordinary microbes into eco-friendly waste cleaners, conveying the concept of "useful engineered microorganisms".
3. **Cultivate engineering thinking**: lead students to identify real-world problems, test flawed solutions, and design biological circuits as innovative alternatives, inspiring problem-solving mindset.
4. **Spark aerospace & synthetic biology enthusiasm**: inspire rural primary students to explore frontier science and motivate their interest in future STEM research.

### Supplementary Interactive Experience

We shared a mini-game *Lunar Mission: Thirty Days Survival* with accessible online links, allowing students to review the mission plot and consolidate knowledge after class. The entire activity delivered accessible, vivid synthetic biology education to students in rural coastal primary schools, fulfilling our team's public science outreach mission of iGEM.

## Programme 2: Outreach at Shenzhen Experimental School (Junior High Section)

### Project Overview

On the afternoon of 14 October 2026, our team carried out a synthetic biology education session at Shenzhen Experimental School (Junior High Section) for 42 students in Grades 7 and 8. The team on site consisted of Shangru Wu, Denghuirong Wang and Mofei He, led by our supervising teacher Yuqian Huang. Built around Friskoli, the session translated the project's central concept, **chemotaxis**, into something students could safely run with their own hands, followed by a board game and a paired pre/post evaluation. A total of 32 of the 42 students returned the paired questionnaire (76%).

We had originally planned a real bacterial chemotaxis assay with the class. We replaced it, and the replacement itself became the lesson: this section therefore focuses on the substitution, its rationale, and what the survey data showed, rather than repeating the science narrative told in Programme 1.

### Keeping the Principle, Changing the Parts: the Brine Shrimp Analogue

A live bacterial chemotaxis assay was our original plan, and we abandoned it for three reasons:

1. **Biosafety**: the audience was minors in a school laboratory without BSL-1 supervision; bringing live engineered *E. coli* into that room was not acceptable.
2. **Time**: a bacterial assay needs overnight culture and a delayed readout; one class period cannot contain it.
3. **Materials**: capillary tubes, microscopes and incubators were not available in the numbers a class of 42 would need.

Instead of cancelling the experiment, we replaced the organism while keeping the concept intact. Brine shrimp nauplii (*Artemia*) show strong positive phototaxis: in a shallow dish with one half illuminated, they migrate toward the light within minutes. The behavioural logic is the same as chemotaxis — biased movement along a gradient toward a favourable cue — only the cue (light instead of cellobiose) and the organism change.

![Artemia cysts](photo_artemia_cysts.jpg)
*Artemia (brine shrimp) cysts used in the assay — aquarium-grade, non-pathogenic, and hatched the day before the session.*

Each group filled a shallow dish, shaded one half with a black card, released the nauplii, and counted the animals in the lit half at 0, 5 and 10 minutes. Groups calculated the percentage in the lit zone at each time point and compared curves. We then asked the question the whole substitution was built for: *if the cue were a sugar instead of light, what would the bacterium be doing?*

![Recording sheet](photo_recording_sheet.jpg)
*A student's drawing of the experimental group: each dot is a brine shrimp, and the lamp sketched at the lower left is the light source. The animals cluster in the zones nearest the lamp.*

The substitution was itself taught as an engineering lesson: engineering is often the work of keeping the principle and changing the parts, and the right question is never "can we do exactly this?" but "what is the smallest change that still answers the question?"

### Educational Goals & Impact

1. **Popularize interdisciplinary knowledge**: connect aerospace microgravity, environmental recycling and microbial genetics in one storyline, for an audience with essentially no prior exposure (96.8% of respondents could not explain bacterial chemotaxis before the session).
2. **Make abstraction observable**: give students a gradient phenomenon they can see, count and plot within one class period.
3. **Cultivate engineering thinking under constraints**: walk through a design that was rejected (mechanical stirring), the reasons it failed, and the criteria a replacement must satisfy.
4. **Measure, not assume**: evaluate the session with a paired pre/post instrument rather than a single post-activity satisfaction form.

### Evaluating the Session: A Voluntary Paired Survey

#### The instrument

We designed *Friskoli Science Field Notes*, a two-sided A4 questionnaire. Students wrote the same anonymous code on both sides, so the two halves could be paired without collecting names. The front side (before the session) recorded baseline familiarity with two topics, three reasoning items and current curiosity; the back side (after) contained five reasoning items, enjoyment, the most helpful session block, curiosity again, and one open comment. Participation was voluntary and ungraded, every question could be skipped, and students who did not fill it in took part in all activities. Of 42 students, 32 returned the paired sheet (76%).

#### Baseline: the audience was genuinely new to the topic

Before the session, 64.5% of respondents (20 of 31) had never encountered bacterial chemotaxis and only one student could roughly explain it; for space resource recycling the numbers were only slightly better. The session was therefore teaching from zero, not revising existing ideas (Figure a).

![Survey results](survey_figure.png)

#### What moved and what did not

The one reasoning item repeated verbatim on both sides (the experimental-design item: how to compare brine shrimp distributions under different lighting) improved from 74.1% to 77.8% among the 27 students who answered both versions, and the share answering "not sure" halved (14.8% → 7.4%). Four students moved from wrong to right and three the other way. The gain was small, and we attribute that to a ceiling effect: the pre-test level was already high (Figure b).

The remaining post-session items were transfer tasks — new scenarios, no practice. The engineering-judgement item (what information is still missing before scaling up a waste-degrading method for a long mission) was answered correctly by 65.4% (17 of 26). The two hardest items, on surface mass transport and on control variables, were answered correctly by 32.0% and 38.5% respectively. We report these as they are: reasoning about invisible gradients and experimental controls is where junior high students currently are, not where a single session can take them.

#### Interest and enjoyment

Enjoyment averaged 4.35 of 5, with 80.6% rating the session 4 or 5 and not a single rating below 3. The share of students who wanted to learn more about microbes or synthetic biology (rating 4–5) rose from 66.7% before the session to 77.4% after; in the 29 students with both halves paired, five increased, 21 stayed the same and three decreased — a modest but consistent shift.

The clearest signal came from the question "which block helped you most": **observing the brine shrimp was chosen by 77.4%**, far ahead of the science explanation (25.8%) and the discussion (12.9%) (Figure c). The hands-on block beat the lecture three to one — direct evidence that our decision to keep a live experiment, even with a substituted organism, was the right call.

#### What students wrote

Twelve students left written comments. Ten were about the brine shrimp: seven restated the core observation in their own words — for example, *"the brine shrimp swam toward the light, and it became more obvious over time"* — and four asked follow-up questions about the animal itself (why they are so small, whether they are toxic). Two praised the board game, one asking for a sequel. Questions about the animal, rather than about bacteria, told us the live material had done its job: it made students curious enough to keep asking after the session ended.

#### A finding we did not expect: the analogue taught the wrong mechanism

On the paired chemotaxis-mechanism item, correct answers *fell* from 36.7% before the session to 16.7% after, and 56.7% of post-session respondents selected "comparing concentrations at both ends of the body and turning toward the higher side" — which is how an animal with light-sensing organs finds a light source, but not how *E. coli* works: a bacterium compares concentration over time and extends its runs when the signal improves. Seven of the eleven students who had answered correctly before the session chose the same letter afterwards, even though that option now carried different content.

We read this as an anchoring effect of our own teaching: the visually dominant brine shrimp taught the behaviour (move up a gradient) so well that students imported the animal's mechanism into the bacterium. The finding does not mean analogues fail; it means an analogue must be dismantled as deliberately as it is introduced.

### The Educational DBTL: What We Change Next Round

1. **Dismantle the analogy explicitly.** Between the shrimp experiment and the bacteria lecture we will add one slide: shrimp sense light with organs and turn directly; *E. coli* has no organs, compares concentration over time, and changes its tumble frequency.
2. **Reduce reading load.** The reactor mass-transfer item will be replaced by a diagram-based item, and the post-test will be cut from five items to three matched with the pre-test. Skipping rose from 6–9% on the front page to about 20% on the text-heaviest back-page items, costing us usable data exactly where the session was hardest to explain.
3. **Fix the instrument artifacts.** Matched items will keep identical option positions or randomized ones — never "same position, different content", which rewards remembering your previous letter over re-reading the question.

### Supplementary Interactive Experience

Alongside the experiment we brought the **Chemotaxis Board Game** designed by our team: each player is an *E. coli* navigating a sugar gradient, with movement cards distinguishing runs from tumbles and obstacle cards for the shear, bubble and heat problems that killed our mechanical-stirring idea. Students replayed it voluntarily and asked for a sequel — the game doubled as a rehearsal of the design decisions behind Friskoli.

## What the Two Programmes Show Together

1. **A tiered curriculum, one storyline.** The 2049 mission narrative carries both audiences: primary students meet it as a story with crafts, junior high students meet it as a design problem with data. The storyline is the constant; the depth of the questions asked about it is the variable.
2. **Constraints breed design lessons.** At Lufeng the constraint was age: abstraction had to become story. In Shenzhen the constraints were biosafety, time and materials: a bacterial assay had to become a brine-shrimp assay. Both substitutions kept the principle and changed the parts — and both were taught to students as exactly that.
3. **Evaluation changes what you see.** The paired survey revealed a failure we would otherwise have celebrated: the shrimp analogue anchored students to the wrong mechanism. That finding now feeds back into Programme 1 as well — younger audiences, if anything, are more prone to importing the animal's mechanism into the bacterium, so the "dismantle the analogy" slide belongs in both decks.
